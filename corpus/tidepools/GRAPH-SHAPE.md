# Graph shape: dense cross-linked garden

- Root count: 1 (`index`).
- Maximum parent depth: 3.
- Fanout: moderate and uneven; maximum fanout is 5 at `index`, with section pages carrying 3–4 children and a few depth-2 concept/habitat pages carrying species accounts.
- Link density: 140 internal links across 28 pages, averaging 5.00 links per page; every page carries exactly 5 internal links.
- Cross-subtree links: 114 of 140 internal links (about 81%) cross the first path segment of their source page, so the link web routinely ignores the parent forest.
- Fragment usage: 140 of 140 internal links include fragments; fragments point at explanatory sections such as `#horizontal-bands`, `#slack-water`, `#keystone-predation`, and `#the-crawlspace-forest`.

Tidepool ecology naturally produces this shape because almost every observation has several simultaneous explanations. A sea star is at once a species account, a keystone predator, a wave-exposure problem, a feeding-time problem, and a storm-aftermath observation. Kelp is habitat, imported food, shelter, evidence of current, and part of a field safety read. Even practical topics such as tide tables, ethics, and observation technique change meaning depending on depth, season, surf, and substrate. A shallow hierarchy is useful for shelving the material, but pretending the knowledge itself is hierarchical would misrepresent the subject; the dense fragment-rich cross-links mirror the way a working naturalist actually moves among physiology, place, timing, and memory.
