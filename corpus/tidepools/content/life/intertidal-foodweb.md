# The Intertidal Food Web

## Who eats whom

The intertidal food web begins with microscopic films, seaweed spores, drifting fragments, and plankton delivered by moving water. Grazers scrape algae, filter feeders strain particles, scavengers convert wounds and debris into meals, and predators rearrange the entire pattern by feeding selectively. [Ochre sea stars](../species/ochre-starfish.md#keystone-predation) can limit mussel dominance; [purple urchins](../species/purple-urchin.md#grazing-pressure) convert kelp and other algae into body mass; and [opaleye](../species/opaleye.md#algae-browser) move through shallow water as flexible omnivores rather than simple herbivores.

A food-web diagram can make these relationships look fixed, but feeding often depends on tide stage. Animals immersed at high tide may be inactive in air, while nocturnal low tides reveal consumers rarely seen by day. This temporal flexibility is why the web should be read beside [keystone species](keystone-species.md#removal-experiments) rather than treated as a static inventory.

## Edges and subsidies

Some of the most important energy arrives from elsewhere. Kelp fragments drift inshore, plankton pulses through with the tide, and birds carry nutrients back to land. Nowhere is that clearer than at [kelp edges](../habitats/kelp-edges.md#where-forest-meets-rock), where offshore production literally washes against intertidal rock. The food web is therefore less a closed loop than a membrane: permeable, seasonal, and often rearranged by storms.
