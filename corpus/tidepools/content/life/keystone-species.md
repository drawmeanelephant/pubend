# Keystone Species and Disproportionate Effects

## Removal experiments

A keystone species has effects larger than its abundance or biomass would predict. The classic rocky-shore example is a predator that prevents one competitor from monopolizing space. On the Pacific coast, [the ochre sea star](../species/ochre-starfish.md#keystone-predation) is famous because experimental removals showed how quickly mussel beds could expand when predation weakened. The lesson is not that one species is important and the rest are scenery; it is that interaction strengths are uneven and sometimes concentrated.

Urchins provide a complementary case. Their grazing can shift algal communities so strongly that the animal becomes an ecosystem engineer by appetite. The pressure described for [purple urchins](../species/purple-urchin.md#grazing-pressure) is most visible where drift kelp is scarce or where predator populations have been altered. In both cases, the keystone concept only becomes meaningful inside [the food web](intertidal-foodweb.md#who-eats-whom).

## Context dependence

Keystone effects are not universal labels. A predator that controls mussels on an exposed bench may matter less in a shallow, sand-scoured pool. On broad rock, for example, the interaction can depend on how long prey remain wet, tying it to [the bench exposure clock](../habitats/rocky-benches.md#exposure-clock). Disturbance also resets the stage; [the storm journal](../journal/storm-aftermath.md#what-the-swell-rearranged) records a shore where physical force temporarily mattered more than predation. The term keystone is therefore a question about context, not a trophy pinned to a species.
