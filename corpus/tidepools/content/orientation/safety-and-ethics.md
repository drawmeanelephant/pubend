# Safety and Ethics on the Shore

## Reading the ocean

The safest tidepooler is usually the one willing to leave early. Begin with the prediction but treat it as a hypothesis: compare the timing of [slack water](../fieldcraft/tide-tables.md#slack-water) with what the sea is actually doing, watch several wave sets before stepping onto outer rock, and keep a dry exit in view. Good [equipment](../fieldcraft/equipment.md#what-to-carry) helps with traction and warmth, but it does not cancel a long-period swell. Narrow [surge channels](../habitats/surge-channels.md#reading-whitewater) deserve special caution because water there accelerates rather than simply rising.

## Leave no trace

Ethics on a crowded shore is mostly restraint repeated many times. Turn small rocks only when necessary and return them exactly, avoid pulling at attached animals, keep hands out of anemone cavities, and do not carry organisms home. The practical rule is to leave the surface so the next observer cannot tell where you stepped. These choices become easier when you understand how brief the safe feeding window can be for intertidal animals; the schedule described in [watching the rise](tide-cycle.md#watch-the-rise) is a biological deadline as well as a human one.

Even apparently loose ground is habitat. On [sand and gravel](../habitats/sand-and-gravel.md#the-mobile-floor), a shell fragment may be shelter, a piece of surfgrass may be food in transit, and a shallow depression may be the only cool microhabitat available during a hot afternoon.
