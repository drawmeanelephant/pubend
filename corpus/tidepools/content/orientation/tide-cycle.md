# Reading the Tide Cycle

## Reading a mixed semidiurnal day

Much of the Pacific coast experiences a mixed semidiurnal tide: two high waters and two low waters in roughly a lunar day, but with unequal heights. The lower low is usually the one that matters for tidepooling, because it exposes benches and pools that remain submerged during the higher low. The practical skill is not memorizing terms; it is translating [tide tables](../fieldcraft/tide-tables.md#slack-water) into the sequence of habitats that will appear and disappear. A negative tide at dawn may open broad rock shelves, while a modest afternoon low may expose only the outer lip of a channel.

The vertical sequence becomes visible as [horizontal bands](zonation.md#horizontal-bands) because each band records a different average balance of wetting, drying, feeding time, and temperature stress. The astronomical prediction is only the first layer, though. Wind, swell period, and barometric pressure can move real water levels enough to matter, which is why [reading the ocean](safety-and-ethics.md#reading-the-ocean) remains part of tide-table literacy.

## Watch the rise

The incoming tide rarely announces itself evenly. Water first threads through low cracks, then reconnects isolated pools, then begins cutting off the rock you crossed an hour earlier. During the equinox visit described in [the spring journal](../journal/spring-equinox.md#when-the-crowd-arrives), the most useful observation was not the lowest water but the moment when small surges started arriving over the outer bench. On broad [rocky benches](../habitats/rocky-benches.md#exposure-clock), that turning point is an exposure clock: it tells you how much longer you can work outward before retreating toward the upper shore.
