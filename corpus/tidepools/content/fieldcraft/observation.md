# Observation Practice

## The first scan

Before touching anything, stop at the upper edge of the area and scan in layers. Note wetness, glare, algae cover, whitewater paths, and the places where water still moves even though the tide appears low. Then scan for scale: large shapes first, then clusters, then individual animals. The method sounds slow, but it prevents the common error of staring at one shell while missing the pattern around it. A small kit helps, as explained in [what to carry](equipment.md#what-to-carry), yet attention is the main instrument.

The scan also makes zonation visible in practice. Compare the height and moisture of each patch with the idealized [horizontal bands](../orientation/zonation.md#horizontal-bands), then ask why the real pattern departs from the diagram. If a crab vanishes under a piece of kelp, resist the urge to chase it; [kelp crabs](../species/kelp-crab.md#not-quite-a-decorator) often freeze when algae moves, and patience reveals more than pursuit.

## Look before touch

Most handling harms are quiet: a foot pressed onto a soft-bodied animal hidden by water, a finger dislodging a limpet whose exact scar matters, or a lifted rock replaced upside down. Watch for feeding posture, current direction, and shadows before considering even gentle contact. Behavioral detail is often what connects an animal to [the food web](../life/intertidal-foodweb.md#who-eats-whom). At night, when beams compress attention, the advice in [lights and shadows](../journal/night-low-tide.md#lights-and-shadows) becomes a stricter version of the same rule.
