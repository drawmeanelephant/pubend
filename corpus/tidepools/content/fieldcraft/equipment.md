# Equipment That Earns Its Weight

## What to carry

The best kit is small enough that you still watch the shore instead of managing objects. Shoes with real tread matter most because intertidal rock is polished by algae, barnacle edges, and invisible films of diatoms. Add a hand lens, a pencil, a notebook in a waterproof sleeve, a small thermometer if you enjoy microclimates, and gloves only where local rules and common sense allow them. Nothing in the kit should encourage handling; [leave-no-trace practice](../orientation/safety-and-ethics.md#leave-no-trace) begins by making passive observation easier than interference.

A flashlight is useful even in daylight because deep cracks hold animals that never present themselves in open view. At night it becomes central, but [the night journal](../journal/night-low-tide.md#lights-and-shadows) shows why a narrow beam is often better than flooding the whole pool. Binoculars can also serve as a safety tool by letting you inspect whitewater in [surge channels](../habitats/surge-channels.md#reading-whitewater) before committing to a route.

## Packing for restraint

A small bucket is useful only when the intent is temporary, careful observation of a mobile animal already detached by wave action; it is not a collecting license. The more reliable instruments are patience and posture. Kneeling low changes reflections, reveals undersides, and slows your movements. Pair the kit with [the first scan](observation.md#the-first-scan), and remember that benches with many visible steps, described under [rocky bench structure](../habitats/rocky-benches.md#benches-as-stair-steps), reward standing still longer than carrying more gear.
