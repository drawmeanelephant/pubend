# Using Tide Tables Without Worshipping Them

## Slack water

Slack water is the turning interval when the tidal current momentarily weakens before reversing. In rocky intertidal work it is rarely a precise minute and never a guarantee of safety. The useful habit is to compare predictions for several nearby stations, then translate them into local effects using [the mixed semidiurnal pattern](../orientation/tide-cycle.md#reading-a-mixed-semidiurnal-day). A low prediction at a protected station may arrive earlier or later along an exposed point, and the shallowest pool may be influenced more by wave wash than by the mean water level.

Tables become powerful when they help you plan a sequence: arrive before the low, work outward as water recedes, and reverse before the first strong returning surge. That schedule underlies [the equinox outing](../journal/spring-equinox.md#when-the-crowd-arrives), where an excellent prediction still had to be reconciled with parking lots, sunlight, and other visitors.

## Predictions and real water

Weather distorts simple expectations. Onshore wind can pile water against the coast, low atmospheric pressure can raise levels, and long-period swell can make an apparently gentle low tide hazardous. This is why tide-table literacy includes [reading the ocean](../orientation/safety-and-ethics.md#reading-the-ocean) rather than stopping at a number. Along [kelp edges](../habitats/kelp-edges.md#where-forest-meets-rock), floating fronds sometimes reveal current direction before it is obvious on open rock, while [the first scan](observation.md#the-first-scan) keeps you oriented to changes instead of the plan you carried in.
