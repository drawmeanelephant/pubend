# Orientation to the Intertidal

The intertidal is easiest to read when its separate clocks are distinguished. The astronomical tide sets the broad schedule, but rock slope, wave exposure, and the depth of each basin decide what that schedule means on a particular shore. A bench that looks safely dry may still be washed by a long-period set, and a pool that looks isolated may exchange water through a hidden crack. This section gathers the mental maps needed before species names become useful: [the tide cycle](orientation/tide-cycle.md#reading-a-mixed-semidiurnal-day), [zonation](orientation/zonation.md#horizontal-bands), and [ethical judgment](orientation/safety-and-ethics.md#leave-no-trace).

Orientation is not a prerequisite in the strict sense; it is a habit. Even experienced observers recalibrate whenever the substrate changes from shelf to channel or when sand movement alters the depth of familiar pools. [Rocky benches](habitats/rocky-benches.md#benches-as-stair-steps) make vertical bands especially legible, while animals such as [the ochre sea star](species/ochre-starfish.md#keystone-predation) reveal how biological interactions can sharpen or blur those bands. Expect the patterns to be real but never mechanical.
