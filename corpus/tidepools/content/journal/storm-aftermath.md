# Storm Aftermath

## What the swell rearranged

Three days after a large swell, the shore looked familiar from the trail and wrong up close. Gravel had been moved into the mouth of a channel, a shallow pool had vanished under sand, and kelp pieces were draped across rock that normally carried only small algae. The channel margins showed why [reading whitewater](../habitats/surge-channels.md#reading-whitewater) is a skill learned on calm days but needed after storms: the same routes were present, yet their energy and debris had changed completely.

On the outer platform, patches of bare rock interrupted the mussel bed. The disturbance gave a physical glimpse of what [the bench exposure clock](../habitats/rocky-benches.md#exposure-clock) usually expresses biologically: time since wetting, time since scouring, and time available for recolonization. A few [ochre sea stars](../species/ochre-starfish.md#keystone-predation) remained wedged in protected corners, motionless but intact.

## Caution in the aftermath

The lesson was not that storms simply damage the shore. They redistribute opportunity. Grazers find fresh film, scavengers find wreckage, and predators encounter prey in unfamiliar positions. That rearrangement is one reason [adaptations to surf](../life/adaptations.md#hanging-on) favor flexibility. It is also why post-storm visits require extra attention to [ocean behavior](../orientation/safety-and-ethics.md#reading-the-ocean): unstable footing and residual swell are a poor combination.
