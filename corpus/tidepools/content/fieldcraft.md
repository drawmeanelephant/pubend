# Fieldcraft for Tidepool Observation

Good tidepooling is mostly the art of arriving early, moving slowly, and leaving the shore unchanged. Equipment matters, but only because the right small objects make patience easier: shoes that grip algae-covered rock, a hand lens, a notebook that can survive spray, and clothing that accepts a sudden soak. The pages here cover [what to carry](fieldcraft/equipment.md#what-to-carry), how to read [slack water](fieldcraft/tide-tables.md#slack-water), and why [the first scan](fieldcraft/observation.md#the-first-scan) often tells you more than the first animal you find.

Fieldcraft also includes knowing when not to go. A low tide does not make a dangerous sea calm, and a sunny afternoon does not make algae less slippery. The safety notes on [reading the ocean](orientation/safety-and-ethics.md#reading-the-ocean) belong in the same mental pocket as the tide table. At night the tradeoffs change again: [lights and shadows](journal/night-low-tide.md#lights-and-shadows) can reveal shy animals while also hiding the edge of a channel. Treat every technique as a way to increase attention, not control.
