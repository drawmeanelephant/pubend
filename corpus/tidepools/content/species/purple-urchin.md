# Purple Sea Urchin

## Grazing pressure

Purple sea urchins, *Strongylocentrotus purpuratus*, are small compared with the ecological effects they can collectively produce. Their scraping teeth remove algal film, young kelp, and drift fragments from rock. At moderate densities this grazing is part of the normal turnover in [the intertidal food web](../life/intertidal-foodweb.md#who-eats-whom). At high densities, especially where predators are scarce or kelp supply is disrupted, urchins can maintain patches of closely cropped rock that resist rapid algal recovery.

Their effect is most instructive at [kelp edges](../habitats/kelp-edges.md#where-forest-meets-rock), because the boundary between forest and open rock is partly a feeding frontier. Urchins do not need to destroy a mature forest directly to influence it; removing recruits and consuming drift material can change what the next season looks like. This is one of the clearest cases for the contextual thinking in [keystone species](../life/keystone-species.md#removal-experiments).

## Pits and persistence

Purple urchins often occupy shallow pits in soft rock, sometimes deepening them over time. The pit is shelter, feeding station, and constraint all at once. After storms, displaced urchins may appear in pools or gravel pockets far from their usual positions, as noted in [the storm journal](../journal/storm-aftermath.md#what-the-swell-rearranged). Their height on the shore is meaningful but not rigid, so compare observations with [horizontal bands](../orientation/zonation.md#horizontal-bands) rather than assuming a fixed address.
