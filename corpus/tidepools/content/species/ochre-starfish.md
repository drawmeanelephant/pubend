# Ochre Sea Star

## Keystone predation

The ochre sea star, *Pisaster ochraceus*, is the animal most often used to explain keystone predation on Pacific rocky shores. It pries open mussels, barnacles, and other prey with persistent force rather than speed. Where sea stars are active, mussels are often prevented from sweeping downward across every available surface, leaving room for algae and smaller invertebrates. That dynamic is the concrete example behind [removal experiments](../life/keystone-species.md#removal-experiments), but the animal itself is more interesting than the abstraction.

Sea stars work slowly because their hydraulic tube feet trade power for time. On exposed benches they can continue hunting only while conditions remain wet enough, tying their feeding to [the exposure clock](../habitats/rocky-benches.md#exposure-clock). Their position in [the food web](../life/intertidal-foodweb.md#who-eats-whom) is therefore temporal as well as trophic.

## Reading a slow predator

Look for the slight arch of the central disk when a sea star is feeding, or for the way its arms conform to irregular rock. After heavy surf, individuals may be wedged into crevices rather than crossing open surfaces, a pattern noted in [the storm-aftermath journal](../journal/storm-aftermath.md#what-the-swell-rearranged). Even when the animal appears inert, it is still negotiating water loss, temperature, and the returning flow described in [watching the rise](../orientation/tide-cycle.md#watch-the-rise).
