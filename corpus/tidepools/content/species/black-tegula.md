# Black Tegula Snail

## Heat and desiccation

The black tegula, *Tegula funebralis*, is easy to overlook because it often gathers in dark seams and shallow pits rather than displaying itself in open water. Those aggregations are part of its response to heat and drying. Shell shape, mucus, posture, and the microclimate of crowded crevices all slow desiccation. The snail is therefore a compact lesson in the tradeoffs described under [heat and desiccation](../life/adaptations.md#heat-and-desiccation), where survival depends less on dramatic defenses than on small physical margins.

Its position is also seasonal and behavioral. On cool, wet rock it may spread across surfaces to graze; during hot exposure it retreats or clusters. Comparing those movements with idealized [horizontal bands](../orientation/zonation.md#horizontal-bands) shows why zonation is a statistical pattern rather than a fixed map. The same rock can hold different snail distributions at noon and dusk.

## Small-scale field signs

Look for mucus trails on damp rock, dense clusters at crevice lips, and individuals oriented tightly against irregularities. On broad platforms the snails make the bench's microtopography legible, reinforcing the link between [the exposure clock](../habitats/rocky-benches.md#exposure-clock) and behavior. During the crowded equinox tide in [the spring journal](../journal/spring-equinox.md#when-the-crowd-arrives), their aggregations were easier to observe than more mobile animals precisely because they did not vanish when people approached. They reward the slow scan described in [the first scan](../fieldcraft/observation.md#the-first-scan).
