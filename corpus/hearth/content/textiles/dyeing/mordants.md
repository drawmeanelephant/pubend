# Mordants

A mordant prepares fiber to hold dye. The word covers several substances, but in a small household studio the practical choices are usually alum for a clear, relatively gentle mordant and iron for darkening or saddening colors that are already understood. Mordanting is not a decorative step. It changes the bond between dye and fiber, affecting washfastness, lightfastness, handle, and color. Too little mordant gives weak results; too much can make wool harsh or brittle.

Accurate measurement is essential. Weigh the dry fiber, calculate the mordant as a percentage of that weight, dissolve the mordant thoroughly, and record the actual quantities rather than the intended ones. The two most useful detailed notes here are [alum mordant](mordants/alum.md) and [iron modifier](mordants/iron.md). Alum is often the baseline for wool and silk, while iron is better treated as a restrained modifier than as a routine first choice. For vat dyes, especially indigo, mordanting follows different logic; consult the [indigo vat](indigo-vat.md#refreshing-the-vat) notes before combining methods.

Mordant work rewards the same patient cleanliness described in [natural dyeing](../dyeing.md). Use nonreactive pots, stir gently, avoid sudden temperature changes, and label fiber before it enters the liquid. Spent baths should be disposed of according to local guidance rather than poured casually into soil or drains. Craft tradition is not a substitute for safety, particularly when children, pets, or food preparation spaces share the household.
