# Indigo Vat

An indigo vat is a living piece of dye chemistry. Indigo pigment is not water soluble in its blue oxidized form, so the vat must first reduce it to a soluble yellow-green state. Cloth or yarn enters gently, absorbs the reduced dye, and then turns blue in the air as oxygen returns. The transformation is dramatic, but success depends more on preparation and patience than on spectacle. Fiber should be clean, fully wet, and lowered without splashing because excess oxygen disturbs the vat.

A small vat can be maintained for months when it is fed and rested correctly. Surface bubbles, color of the liquid, smell, and the behavior of a test strip all provide evidence. Do not judge a vat only by the intensity of the first dip. Pale blue is often the beginning of a layered color built through repeated short dips rather than one long immersion. The main [natural dyeing](../dyeing.md) page explains why records matter, and [mordants](mordants.md) clarifies when conventional mordanting is or is not appropriate.

## Refreshing the vat

When blues become weak or the liquid looks tired, first check temperature and reduction before adding more indigo. Gentle stirring, rest, and measured additions of the reducing agent specified by the chosen vat recipe often restore balance. Keep a log of every addition. A vat that is corrected in small steps is easier to understand than one rescued by a handful of guesses. After dipping, rinse fiber until the water runs clearer, dry it away from direct harsh sun, and only then judge whether another dip is needed.
