# Iron Modifier

Iron is best approached as a modifier rather than a universal mordant. Small amounts shift yellows toward olive, reds toward brown or plum, and tannin-rich dyes toward gray or black. Those effects can be beautiful, especially for utility cloth, but iron also hardens the hand of wool and can weaken fibers when used heavily. The practical rule is restraint: use the smallest amount that produces the desired shift, apply it after an initial dye bath when possible, and test on samples before committing a full skein.

Because iron is reactive, tools matter. A dedicated stainless or enamel pot, a nonreactive spoon, and careful rinsing prevent unwanted marks in later dye work. Even a trace of iron left in a vessel can alter a delicate yellow. Keep iron-modified samples clearly labeled and compare them with untreated samples after washing and exposure to indirect light. The comparison belongs with the broader notes on [mordants](../mordants.md), not as an isolated recipe.

Iron also illustrates why dyeing is a system rather than a collection of secrets. The same fiber preparation, water, and temperature that produce a clear alum-mordanted color can produce a dull or brittle result once iron is introduced. Before using it in a garment yarn, review [hand spinning](../../hand-spinning.md) with durability in mind and consider whether the cloth will face abrasion in use. The [indigo vat](../indigo-vat.md) follows different chemistry and should not be treated as another iron-modified dye bath.
