# Mending

Mending keeps household textiles useful and makes wear visible without treating it as failure. A darn is not merely a patch of thread; it is a reconstructed piece of cloth that must flex, wash, and abrade in sympathy with the original fabric. The best repair is therefore chosen after examining the weave or knit, the direction of stress, and the expected life of the garment. A work shirt elbow, a sock heel, and a wool blanket edge ask for different structures even when the hole is the same size.

Woven cloth often accepts a patch or running darn, while knitted fabric usually needs duplicate stitch or a knitted-in reconstruction. Matching fiber matters more than matching color. A bright patch in compatible wool can outlast an invisible patch in weak cotton. Handspinners can use leftover singles or purpose-spun repair yarn; the notes on [hand spinning](hand-spinning.md) are useful when matching twist and thickness. Clean fiber preparation, described in [fiber preparation](fiber-preparation.md), also matters because greasy or dirty repair yarn behaves differently in washing.

Simple wooden tools make the work easier. A darning mushroom or smooth egg supports the cloth without stretching it, and its surface should be finished so it neither splinters nor sheds oil into fabric. The woodshop page on [finishes](../woodshop/finishes.md) explains suitable low-toxicity surface treatments. Mending is also where dye knowledge returns in practical form: a faded garment can be patched with cloth tinted in the tone described in [natural dyeing](dyeing.md), making the repair deliberate rather than apologetic.
