# Natural Dyeing

Natural dyeing is a disciplined experiment disguised as a kitchen craft. Water quality, fiber cleanliness, mordant choice, dye material, temperature, and time all affect the result. A page of careful notes is worth more than a vivid memory, especially when a color must be repeated for enough yarn to finish a garment. The safest approach is to work small, label every sample, and change only one variable at a time. Plant dyes can be beautiful and lightfast, but they are not magic; they are chemistry practiced with humble materials.

Most protein fibers benefit from a mordant, a soluble metal salt or traditional preparation that helps dye bond to fiber. The dedicated [mordants](dyeing/mordants.md) page compares alum and iron in household use. Indigo behaves differently because the pigment is reduced in a vat and oxidizes in the air after the cloth is removed; see the [indigo vat](dyeing/indigo-vat.md) notes before attempting one. Fiber must first be clean and evenly wet, which makes the washing guidance in [fiber preparation](fiber-preparation.md) part of the dye process rather than a separate concern.

## Mordant before color

The order of operations matters. In most household dyeing the fiber is scoured, mordanted, rinsed or rested, and only then dyed. Wood ash has also been used historically as an alkaline modifier in some dye traditions. Its variability is the same reason ash must be tested carefully in ceramics, as discussed in the cross-craft notes on [ash glaze](../ceramics/glazes/ash-glaze.md). Keep mordant and dye pots dedicated to craft use, never food use, even when the ingredients sound benign.
