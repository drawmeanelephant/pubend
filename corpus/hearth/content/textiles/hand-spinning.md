# Hand Spinning

Hand spinning turns prepared fiber into yarn through controlled twist. The beginner's task is not speed but consistency: a steady draft, a predictable amount of twist, and a rhythm that allows the hands to correct small irregularities before they become thick-and-thin surprises. A drop spindle is an excellent first tool because it makes the mechanics visible. A spinning wheel adds efficiency, but it does not remove the need to understand staple length, drafting zone, and the difference between a slub and a deliberate texture.

Yarn design begins before the first draft. A firm worsted single intended for weaving needs a different preparation and twist angle than a soft woolen yarn meant for a warm knitted cap. Samples matter. Spin a short length, ply it, wash it, and handle it dry before committing several hundred grams of fiber. The notes in [fiber preparation](fiber-preparation.md) explain how carded and combed preparations influence the final yarn, while [mending](mending.md) shows why durability is sometimes more valuable than softness in household cloth.

Wheel maintenance is part of the craft. Drive bands stretch, hooks collect dust, and wooden moving parts benefit from occasional cleaning and an appropriate surface finish. Spinners who intend to dye yarn should keep skeins loosely tied in several places before washing them; tangled skeins waste time in the dye pot. The [natural dyeing](dyeing.md) section includes notes on preparing skeins so color reaches them evenly.
