# Glazes

A glaze is a thin layer of glass-forming material that melts and bonds to ceramic during firing. It is chemistry, surface design, and food-safety responsibility in one bucket. A glaze can look beautiful and still be unsuitable for functional ware if it crazes, leaches, or remains too rough to clean. Recipe discipline is therefore non-negotiable. Weigh materials, mix batches consistently, sieve when required, label containers, and keep test tiles for every clay body and firing schedule in use.

Glaze application is as important as formulation. Bisque ware must be clean and porous enough to accept glaze evenly. Dipping time, glaze thickness, wax-resist placement, and drying time all change the fired surface. Too thin and the color starves; too thick and the glaze runs onto shelves or pinholes. A detailed example is the studio's [ash glaze](glazes/ash-glaze.md), which demonstrates why ingredient variability must be tested rather than romanticized. Clay body behavior, covered in [clay preparation](clay-preparation.md), also affects glaze fit.

Functional pottery requires conservative judgment. Shiny does not mean safe, and a long tradition of use does not make lead, cadmium, or unstable ash glazes acceptable for food contact. When a glaze is uncertain, reserve the piece for decorative use or discard it. The firing process described in [firing](firing.md) completes the glaze, but no kiln schedule can correct a badly formulated recipe. Keep failures as labeled evidence, not as ambiguous gifts.
