# Clay Preparation

Clay preparation aligns moisture, particle distribution, and plasticity so the material responds predictably on the wheel or workbench. Reclaimed clay must be sorted by body type, slaked down, dried to a workable consistency, and wedged thoroughly enough to remove air pockets. New clay also benefits from wedging, especially if it has stiffened unevenly in storage. The object being made determines how soft the clay should be. Large vessels need enough stiffness to hold their walls; small cups tolerate softer clay.

Wedging is both mixing and inspection. Hard lumps, bits of plaster, dry crumbs, and trapped air reveal themselves under repeated pressure. Spiral wedging and ram's-head wedging are different means to the same result; consistency matters more than style. The work surface should be clean, slightly absorbent, and reserved from contaminants. Wooden wedging tables and plaster bats need regular maintenance, so they should be inspected with the same seriousness as the clay itself.

Prepared clay should be stored in airtight containers or bags, labeled by body and date. Reclaim should never be mixed casually across clay bodies because firing temperature, color, and vitrification behavior differ. Before a piece is glazed or fired, the clay history matters. The next decisions belong to [glazes](glazes.md) and [firing](firing.md), but those stages cannot rescue clay that was inconsistent at the start. Good preparation is invisible in the finished vessel precisely because it prevents drama.
