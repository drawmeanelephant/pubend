# Firing

Firing turns shaped clay into ceramic through controlled heatwork. The kiln schedule is not merely a temperature number. Rate of rise, hold time, atmosphere, shelf position, load density, and cooling all affect the result. A piece can be underfired at the same peak temperature if the kiln climbed too quickly, or overfired in a hot spot even when the controller reading looked correct. Witness cones, careful loading, and written firing logs turn each kiln opening into usable evidence.

Preparation begins before the kiln is lit. Pieces must be dry enough for the schedule, feet must be clean, and glazed bottoms must not threaten shelves. Posts and shelves should be inspected for cracks or glaze drips. Leave enough space for heat circulation. Do not treat a crowded kiln as a test of optimism. Clay preparation affects firing behavior, especially trapped air and uneven thickness, so the cautions in [clay preparation](clay-preparation.md) remain relevant until the door is closed. Glaze movement and fit, discussed in [glazes](glazes.md), become visible here.

Cooling is part of firing. Opening too early can crack ware through thermal shock and can mislead judgment because colors and surfaces continue changing as they cool. Record when the kiln is opened and compare witness cones from different positions. The [ash glaze](glazes/ash-glaze.md#reading-the-kiln) notes show how movement and heatwork interact in one concrete glaze family. Functional pieces must then be evaluated honestly, as described in [functional forms](functional-forms.md), before entering household use.
