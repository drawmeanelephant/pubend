# Ash Glaze

Ash glaze uses washed or unwashed wood ash as a significant source of fluxing material. It is attractive because the ingredient connects the kiln to local fuel and household ashes, but that same origin makes it unpredictable. Ash composition varies by wood species, soil, season, combustion temperature, and even how thoroughly the ash is sieved. A bucket of ash is not a standardized ceramic material. Treat every new source as a new glaze ingredient requiring tests.

Washing ash removes some soluble salts and reduces caustic behavior, but it also changes the chemistry. Record whether ash is washed, how many times, and how long it settled before decanting. Wear appropriate protection because dry ash is alkaline and irritating. Sieve out charcoal and coarse debris, then mix the glaze by weight rather than volume. Small line blends are the responsible way to learn a new ash source. Compare fired results on the same clay body and shelf position before changing more than one variable.

## Reading the kiln

Ash glazes often run when they are hot enough to develop depth. Test tiles should include a catch cookie or sacrificial tile beneath them, and functional pieces need generous foot clearance. The relationship between heatwork and movement is described further in [firing](../firing.md). Ash chemistry also crosses into other alkaline household crafts in a cautionary way: dyers once used ash liquors as modifiers, but the variability discussed here is exactly why modern dyers measure carefully, as noted in [natural dyeing](../../textiles/dyeing.md). Local material is a starting point for testing, never a guarantee.
