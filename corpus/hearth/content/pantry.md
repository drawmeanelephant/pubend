# Pantry

The pantry collection treats preservation as a chain of decisions rather than a pile of recipes. Food enters in season, is sorted honestly, and is preserved by the method best suited to its water content, acidity, texture, and intended use. Fermentation transforms flavor while creating inhospitable conditions for many spoilage organisms. Canning uses heat and controlled acidity for shelf stability. Drying removes enough water that microbial growth becomes difficult. Each method has a different margin for error and deserves its own discipline.

Begin with the method rather than the harvest glut. The [fermentation](pantry/fermentation.md) notes emphasize salt, cleanliness, and observation; [canning](pantry/canning.md) insists on tested recipes because low-acid food is unforgiving; [drying](pantry/drying.md) depends on airflow more than enthusiasm. The pantry is also a material system. Crocks, jars, weights, racks, and storage shelves determine whether the work remains orderly after the first excited batch. Hand-built vessels can be part of that system when chosen carefully, provided their clay, glaze, and shape are suited to food use.

A good pantry record names the produce, source, date, recipe, batch size, and final result. These notes make preservation repeatable and expose patterns: one apple variety collapses when dried, one cabbage ferments too fast in a warm room, one jar size never gets opened. The point is not austerity. It is confidence that the food put by in abundance will be safe, pleasant, and actually eaten.
