# Canning

Canning preserves food through heat processing in sealed jars, and its safety depends on controlled acidity, jar preparation, headspace, processing time, and altitude adjustment. High-acid foods such as properly acidified fruits and pickles can be processed in boiling water. Low-acid vegetables, meats, and many mixed foods require a pressure canner and a tested recipe. This is not an area for improvisation. A sealed jar can look perfect while hiding dangerous microbial growth, including botulism.

The safest household habit is to separate creativity from processing. Flavor experiments can happen within the boundaries of tested recipes, but jar size, ingredient density, acidity, and processing time must not be casually altered. Use jars free of chips, lids within their intended reuse limits, and a canner large enough for water or pressure to circulate as directed. Label every jar with contents and date. Store it without the ring so a failed seal is obvious. When in doubt, throw it out without tasting.

Canning is one branch of the pantry rather than the whole discipline. Fermented foods such as [sauerkraut](fermentation/sauerkraut.md#processing-for-the-shelf) may be heat processed when a tested procedure exists, but refrigeration often preserves better texture. [Drying food](drying.md) is safer and more appropriate for many herbs, fruits, and mushrooms. The general preservation overview in [fermentation](fermentation.md) and the pantry introduction help match method to ingredient. Good canning is quiet, exact work: no drama, no improvisation, and no jars kept because throwing away food feels wasteful.
