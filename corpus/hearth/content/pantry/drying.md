# Drying Food

Drying preserves food by lowering available moisture until spoilage organisms cannot easily grow. Success depends less on heat than on airflow, thin and uniform pieces, and an honest assessment of dryness. Herbs may dry in a shaded room with moving air, while juicy fruit often needs a dehydrator or carefully managed oven. Mushrooms, apples, tomatoes, and leafy herbs all behave differently. The goal is not to cook the food but to remove water before quality deteriorates.

Preparation begins with sorting. Bruised or moldy pieces should not enter the drying batch. Cut pieces evenly, pretreat only when the food and method call for it, and spread material in a single layer. Air must move around every surface, which is why slatted racks and mesh screens matter. A simple wooden rack can be built as a small joinery project, but it must be made from clean, unfinished or appropriately finished material and kept away from dust, solvents, and insects.

Drying is also a textile-adjacent skill in household infrastructure: fiber preparation depends on similar attention to airflow and moisture, though the equipment and hygiene standards should remain separate. Once food is dry, condition it by loosely filling a jar and checking for condensation over several days. If moisture appears, dry the batch further. Store containers in a cool dark place, label them, and use the oldest first. Compared with [canning](canning.md), drying is forgiving in some ways, but moldy dried food is still a failure, not a rustic imperfection.
