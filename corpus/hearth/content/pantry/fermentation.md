# Fermentation

Fermentation preserves food by encouraging desirable microbes to transform it before undesirable organisms gain control. In vegetable ferments, salt concentration, temperature, oxygen exposure, and cleanliness shape the outcome. The process is ancient, but it is not careless. Hands, vessels, weights, and produce should be clean; vegetables should stay submerged; and the ferment should be observed rather than hidden for a month and hoped over. Good fermentation looks active and smells appetizingly sour, not rotten or slimy.

The core vegetable example here is [sauerkraut](fermentation/sauerkraut.md), a preparation simple enough for beginners and sensitive enough to teach important habits. Vessel choice matters because acids and salt interact with some materials. The notes on [fermentation crocks](fermentation/crocks.md) explain water-sealed and open crocks, weights, and the practical difference between traditional equipment and improvised containers. Not every preserved food should be fermented, and not every ferment belongs on the shelf unprocessed. For comparison with heat preservation, read [canning](canning.md) before deciding how a finished ferment will be stored.

Fermentation is seasonal kitchen work. Cooler rooms slow fermentation and often produce firmer texture; warm rooms accelerate acidity but can soften vegetables. Record the temperature range as well as the salt percentage. Drying, covered in [drying food](drying.md), preserves a different set of ingredients and can complement fermentation when a harvest is too large for one method. A resilient pantry uses each process where it is strongest instead of forcing one recipe to solve every surplus.
