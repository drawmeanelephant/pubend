# Joinery

Joinery is the discipline of making separate pieces of wood act as one useful object. Good joints manage wood movement, create mechanical strength, and present enough gluing or bearing surface for the load they carry. They also record decisions. A shoulder line, a knife wall, and a cleanly struck mortise show that the maker understood where precision mattered and where a surface could remain quietly imperfect. Practice joints are not wasted time; they are controlled experiments in fitting and repair.

The two core studies in this collection are [mortise and tenon](joinery/mortise-and-tenon.md) and [dovetails](joinery/dovetails.md). The first teaches frame strength and accurate internal surfaces; the second teaches layout, sawing to a line, and the visual rhythm of interlocking parts. Both depend on the preparation described in [tool setup](tool-setup.md) and the edge condition described in [sharpening](sharpening.md). Neither should be rushed into a finished piece before a small test joint has been cut in similar wood.

Joinery is practical beyond furniture. A slatted rack for drying herbs or fruit can be a simple frame-and-panel exercise, and the pantry's [drying food](../pantry/drying.md) notes explain the airflow such a rack must provide. Utility projects are often better teachers than decorative ones because they expose weak joints quickly. If a rack twists or a stool loosens, examine the failure rather than hiding it. Joinery improves through that honest feedback.
