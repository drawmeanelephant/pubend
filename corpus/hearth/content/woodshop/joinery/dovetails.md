# Dovetails

Dovetails resist pulling apart because their tapered shapes interlock mechanically. They are common in drawer boxes, chests, and small carcases, but their first lesson is not furniture history. It is accuracy under repetition. Each tail or pin must be laid out in relation to its neighbor, sawn to the waste side of a line, and pared without crushing the arrises. A well-cut dovetail is both structure and evidence of attention.

There is honest disagreement about whether to cut tails or pins first. What matters is choosing one order, learning its transfer method, and using it consistently. Mark the baseline with a knife, orient the board clearly, and saw with the grain rather than forcing the blade back after a wandering start. Waste can be removed with a coping saw and chisel, but the final fitting should be restrained. Heavy paring often turns a nearly correct joint into a loose one. Sharp chisels and saws make the joint feel entirely different, so review [sharpening](../sharpening.md) if fibers crush rather than cut.

The dovetail's visual prominence makes gaps conspicuous, but strength still matters more than display. Practice in inexpensive straight-grained stock before attempting decorative hardwood. Compare its behavior with [mortise and tenon](mortise-and-tenon.md): one joint excels at frame construction, the other at box corners. The broader [joinery](../joinery.md) notes emphasize choosing joints according to load and wood movement rather than ornament alone.
