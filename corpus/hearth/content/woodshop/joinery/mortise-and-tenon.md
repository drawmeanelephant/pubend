# Mortise and Tenon

The mortise and tenon joint joins a rail to a post by fitting a projecting tongue into a matching cavity. Its strength comes from accurate cheeks, square shoulders, and enough depth to resist twisting. Layout begins on the face side and face edge so every measurement shares the same references. Knife lines are better than pencil lines where a chisel or saw must register. The joint can be cut with machines, but hand cutting teaches the relationship between grain direction, sharpness, and controlled force.

Cut the mortise first when learning, then fit the tenon to it. Remove waste in small steps, pare vertically from both sides when possible, and avoid levering a chisel against the final walls. The tenon should enter with firm hand pressure or light mallet taps, not as a wedge that splits the post. If the joint is too tight, mark the shiny bearing spots after a test fit and remove only those places. Sharpness, as described in [sharpening](../sharpening.md), is not optional here; end grain exposes every weakness in the edge.

Compared with [dovetails](dovetails.md), the mortise and tenon hides more of its workmanship after assembly. That makes test fitting especially important. The broader [joinery](../joinery.md) page places the joint in frames, doors, racks, and stools. In utility work such as the drying frames used with pantry produce, a small drawbored peg can add security, but only after the basic joint fits correctly. Glue should be treated as insurance, not as compensation for a loose pair of cheeks.
