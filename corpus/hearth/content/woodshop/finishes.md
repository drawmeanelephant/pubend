# Finishes

A finish protects wood from moisture, dirt, and wear while shaping how the surface feels in the hand. The correct choice depends on use. A countertop, a spoon, a chair arm, and a darning mushroom face different kinds of contact. Penetrating oils are easy to renew but offer limited film protection; shellac is fast and repairable but vulnerable to alcohol and heat; hardwax oils balance feel and water resistance for many household objects. The important habit is matching the finish to the object rather than searching for one universal coating.

Surface preparation matters more than the brand on the tin. Dust, oil, and torn fibers show through thin finishes. Scrape or sand only as much as the object requires, remove dust, and test on an offcut from the same board. Thin coats cure more reliably than thick ones. Follow the manufacturer's curing guidance, keep oily rags flat or in a metal container because they can self-heat, and ventilate without stirring dust into the wet surface. A finish schedule should be recorded just as carefully as joinery dimensions.

Woodshop finishes also serve neighboring crafts. Textile tools such as darners and spinning-wheel parts need smooth, clean surfaces, which is why [mending](../textiles/mending.md) benefits from finish choices that will not transfer color or odor. Utensils used near food should receive finishes explicitly intended for food contact. Even then, curing time matters. Review [tool setup](tool-setup.md) for safe storage and keep finishing materials separate from shavings, solvents, and open flame.
