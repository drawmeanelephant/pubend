# Sharpening

Sharpening is the maintenance of a simple geometry: two polished surfaces meeting at a controlled angle. Whether the tool is a chisel, plane iron, knife, or spokeshave blade, the goal is the same. Establish the bevel, remove the burr, and polish enough of the back that the cutting edge is continuous. Expensive stones are less important than flatness, consistent technique, and frequent light maintenance. A blade honed for one minute at the end of a work session rarely needs a major repair.

The first step is diagnosis. A dull edge reflects light, crushes fibers instead of severing them, or requires unusual force. Chips and hollows need coarse work; ordinary dullness does not. Work through only as many abrasive stages as needed, keeping the angle stable and the stone or plate flat. Water stones cut quickly but require flattening; oil stones are slower and cleaner in some shops; abrasive films are portable and consistent. Choose a system small enough that it will actually be used.

Sharp tools change the quality of every downstream task. Mortises pare cleanly, dovetails split accurately, and surfaces need less sanding. The joint pages on [mortise and tenon](joinery/mortise-and-tenon.md) and [dovetails](joinery/dovetails.md) both assume edges that can slice end grain without tearing. Sharpening also protects the user, because controlled cutting is safer than forcing a dull edge. Return occasionally to the broader [tool setup](tool-setup.md) notes so storage and rust prevention do not undo the work at the stone.
