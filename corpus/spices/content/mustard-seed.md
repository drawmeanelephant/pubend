# Mustard Seed

Mustard seeds come in yellow, brown, and black forms, each with a different intensity and culinary role. On their own the seeds are not especially hot; their pungency emerges when they are crushed and mixed with liquid, triggering enzymatic reactions that create mustard's familiar sharpness. In Indian cooking, whole seeds are often popped in hot oil before other ingredients are added, producing a nutty aroma rather than the nasal heat associated with prepared mustard.

The spice has two distinct personalities: whole seeds are crunchy, nutty, and relatively gentle, while ground mustard is penetrating and sharp. [Fenugreek](fenugreek.md) and mustard seed often meet in pickles because both preserve well in acid and oil. Turmeric contributes color and bass notes to the same mixtures, while a leaf of bay laurel can make brines taste more herbal. Once seeds are ground, their chemistry is time-sensitive, so freshly mixed mustard is usually brighter than a mixture left to stand.
