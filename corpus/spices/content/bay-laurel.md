# Bay Laurel

Bay laurel leaves are the quiet, structural spice of stocks, soups, beans, sauces, and braises. Their flavor is herbal, woody, slightly bitter, and faintly eucalyptus-like, but it emerges slowly rather than as an immediate aromatic burst. A bay leaf rarely tastes essential in a single spoonful; its absence becomes obvious in the finished pot, which may seem flatter and less coherent. Dried leaves are standard in most kitchens, while fresh bay is stronger and can be more bitter.

Bay works best with time and moisture. Add it early, keep the leaf count modest, and remove it before serving because even cooked leaves remain stiff. Its subdued bitterness helps frame sharper spices such as [black pepper](black-pepper.md) and mustard seed, especially in broths and pickling liquids. Bay is less directly interchangeable with other spices, but a dish that uses sumac for brightness can still benefit from bay underneath, since acidity and herbal bitterness solve different problems.
