# Abies

Abies, the true firs, bear soft flat needles directly on the twig, usually leaving smooth circular scars when they detach. Many species show strongly two-ranked foliage, with the needles spreading horizontally like the teeth of a comb, though upper-crown shoots can be more crowded or curved. The cones stand upright on the branches and disintegrate at maturity, leaving a narrow central spike. Because intact cones are rarely found on the ground, identification often depends on foliage arrangement, needle-tip shape, resin blisters in young bark, and habitat.

The two species included here occupy different moisture and elevation bands. [Grand fir](abies/grandis.md) is common in productive lowland and lower montane forests, especially west of the Cascade crest. [Pacific silver fir](abies/amabilis.md) favors cooler, wetter mountain slopes and often forms dense stands near the upper forest. Both are shade tolerant when young, so small individuals can persist beneath a canopy for decades before reaching light.
