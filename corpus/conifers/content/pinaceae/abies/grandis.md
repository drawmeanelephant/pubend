# Grand Fir

## Field marks

Grand fir, Abies grandis, is usually recognized by its flat needles arranged in two conspicuous horizontal ranks along lower and shaded branches. The needles are glossy dark green above and marked with pale stomatal bands beneath. Crushed foliage has a resinous citrus-like odor that many field observers find distinctive. Young bark is smooth and gray with resin blisters, becoming thicker and more furrowed with age. As in other true firs, the upright cones disintegrate on the tree, so fallen intact cones should not be expected beneath mature individuals.

## Habitat

This species is widespread in moist lowland and lower montane forests, especially west of the Cascade crest, but it also extends into suitable interior valleys. It grows with Douglas-fir, western hemlock, western redcedar, and bigleaf maple on productive sites. Grand fir is more shade tolerant than Douglas-fir and can regenerate beneath partial canopy cover, though prolonged deep shade slows it considerably. On dry or frequently burned sites it is less persistent, because thin young bark and resinous foliage make smaller trees vulnerable to fire.
